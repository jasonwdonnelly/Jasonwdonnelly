Your Content Isn't Bad. It's Just Invisible.

Your content isn't bad.

I know. That's not the diagnosis you came here for. You've been staring at your numbers wondering what's wrong with your writing. Too long? Wrong topics? Should you have made it a poll?

Stop.

Here's the thing.

Most content doesn't die because it's bad. It dies because nobody ever saw it. It showed up quietly, in a feed full of louder things, said something reasonable in a reasonable tone, and disappeared.

Not rejected. Ignored.

There's a difference, and the difference is the whole game.

Quality is the entry fee, not the strategy

Somewhere along the way we all absorbed a comfortable lie: make good stuff and the audience will come.

They will not come.

They don't know you exist.

The internet is not a meritocracy. It's a crowded room where everyone is talking at once, and the person who gets heard is not the one with the best point. It's the one who understands how rooms work.

I've been writing for brands for 13+ years. Big ones. Ones you keep in your fridge. And in all that time I have watched great work get a few hundred views and die alone. I have also watched lazy work do numbers that would make you weep into your content calendar.

The difference was almost never the quality.

It was the lane and the hook. Good work without those isn't good work.

It's a secret.

Pick a lane. Stay in it until it's boring.

A lane is the thing people can count on you for.

Not a niche you picked from a listicle. A lane. The subject where, when your name shows up, people already know why they should care.

And here's what nobody tells you about lanes: they work for mechanical reasons, not creative ones. When you post about one thing consistently, the people who care about that thing find you, follow you, and come back for more. When you post about seven things, nobody knows why they followed you in the first place.

Neither does the algorithm.

I grew a real chunk of my LinkedIn following by posting about pizza. Daily. For about nine months. Was pizza my great professional calling? No. But it was a lane. I stayed in it long past the point where it felt clever, and people knew exactly what they were getting every single time I showed up.

That's the part everyone skips. The lane only starts working AFTER it gets boring to you. Your audience finds you on repetition two hundred, not repetition nine.

You're bored of your lane. They're just discovering it.

Write that down.

The hook is the whole fight

Now the second half.

A lane gets the right people near your content. The hook is what stops them.

Line one decides everything. Not your argument. Not your credentials. Not the beautiful paragraph four you spent an hour polishing. Nobody who bounced off line one ever met paragraph four.

You get one line. That's the deal you made when you published on the internet instead of in a book someone already paid for.

So try this. Pull up your last five posts and read only the first lines. Would YOU stop scrolling? Or do they all clear their throat? "In today's world." "I've been thinking a lot lately." "Excited to share."

That's not a hook. That's a nap.

A hook is tension. A confession. A flat claim someone could disagree with. A specific number. A comfortable lie, named out loud so you can spend the rest of the post taking it apart.

The hook isn't a trick you put on top of the content. The hook IS the content, compressed into one line and sharpened until it draws blood.

If you can't compress your idea into one line that creates tension, that's usually the real problem. Not the writing.

The idea.

What to actually do

None of this requires talent you don't have. It requires decisions you've been avoiding.

Pick one lane. The overlap of what you know deeply and what your audience actually talks about at work. Not what you talk about in your meetings. What they talk about in theirs.

Stay in it for six months, minimum. Post consistently. Quality over volume, but consistency over everything.

Then spend a stupid amount of time on first lines. Write ten hooks for every post and keep one. It feels wasteful. It's the least wasteful thing you'll do, because the first line is the only part everyone sees.

And stop rewriting the middle of posts nobody opened. You're polishing the inside of a locked house.

The uncomfortable part

Invisible is fixable. That's the good news.

The bad news is that fixing it means admitting the work wasn't the problem. Your favorite excuse was. "The algorithm buried it" is a much more comfortable story than "I gave it a first line that read like a memo."

I say this with love, as someone whose whole job is making brands sound like they have a pulse. Getting seen is a craft. It sits right next to writing, it is not the same thing as writing, and most people refuse to learn it because it feels like marketing.

You're a marketer. It IS marketing. That's allowed.

If you want to see what this looks like when it's my actual day job, the work is right here. Lanes, hooks, and all.

Now go fix your first lines.

Please and thank you. 😘
