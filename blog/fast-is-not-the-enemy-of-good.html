Fast Is Not the Enemy of Good. Bad Is.

I wrote one of my books in a month.

I wrote another one over thirteen years.

Same writer. Same standards. Same flinch when a sentence is bad.

And people have opinions about both. The month book gets "well, it can't be any good then." The thirteen-year book gets "wow, a real labor of love." As if the calendar is the quality.

Here's the thing.

Fast is not the enemy of good. Bad is the enemy of good. And bad shows up on any schedule you like.

The comfortable lie

Somewhere along the way, creative people agreed on a comfortable lie: that time spent equals quality earned. That agonizing is a virtue. That if you suffered long enough over a thing, the suffering transfers into the work like a marinade.

It's a lovely idea.

It's also how you end up with a folder full of "almost ready" projects that are four years old and somehow still not good.

I've spent 13+ years writing for brands. Deadlines that would make a poet cry. And I'll tell you what that pressure taught me that no writing workshop ever did: speed doesn't lower the bar. Speed reveals whether you have one.

If your standards live in your gut, they come with you at any pace. If your standards live in the amount of time you're allowed to fuss, you don't have standards. You have a stalling ritual.

The two disciplines

Because here's what nobody tells you. "Fast" and "patient" aren't opposites. They're two separate disciplines, and good work usually needs both. Just not at the same time.

Discipline one is shipping fast. Getting the thing OUT of your head and onto the page while it's still alive. Drafts love momentum. The first version of anything is better written in a sprint than assembled over six careful weeks, because a draft written slowly is really twelve mismatched drafts wearing a trench coat.

Discipline two is revising slow. This is where patience earns its keep. Not in the making. In the judging. Reading it back cold. Feeling the flinch. Cutting the line you loved because it was showing off. Doing that again next week.

Most people get this exactly backwards.

They draft slowly, second-guessing every sentence while it's being born, and then revise quickly, because by the time they finish they're so sick of the thing they just want it gone.

Slow drafting, fast revising. It's the worst possible order. Write that down.

What speed actually costs

I'm not going to stand here and tell you speed is free. It isn't.

Fast work costs you the fantasy. The fantasy that the thing in your head is a masterpiece, kept perfect by never being finished. Shipping fast murders that fantasy every single time, because now the work exists and you have to look at it.

That's the real reason people work slow. Not craft. Protection.

An unfinished project can't fail. It can only be "in progress." And "in progress" feels so much better than "published and ignored" that people will stretch a six-week project across four years just to keep living there.

I get it. I've lived there. The novel that took me thirteen years wasn't thirteen years of writing. It was a couple years of writing wearing a very thick coat of avoiding.

The month book was avoidance with the exits blocked. A deadline so tight the fantasy never had time to move in. Just draft, flinch, cut, rebuild, done.

Was the fast one worse? No. It was more honest. It's exactly as good as I actually was that month, with nowhere to hide.

How to tell fast from bad

So how do you know if you're being fast or just being sloppy? Simple test.

Fast work still gets read back out loud. Sloppy work gets sent.

Fast work still cuts the weak line. Sloppy work keeps it because "it's fine."

Fast work has a bar it refuses to go under, no matter the deadline. Sloppy work has a deadline and calls it a bar.

The difference was never the clock. It's whether anyone's home when the work gets judged. You can be home in an afternoon. You can be absent for a decade.

The part where I tell you what to do

If you've got a project that's been "almost ready" for more than a year, I'm going to hold your hands when I say this: it's not marinating. It's hiding. And you're the one hiding it.

Give it a deadline that scares you a little. Draft like the building's on fire. Then, and only then, slow all the way down and judge it like you have all the time in the world.

Fast hands. Patient eyes.

That's the whole system. That's the two disciplines. Everything else is a stalling ritual with better branding.

The month book is sitting on the shelf right now. The thirteen-year novel is about to join it. And readers will judge them both without ever asking how long they took.

That's the punchline, by the way. Nobody experiences your process. They experience your work.

The calendar was never the point. The bar is.

Now go finish something. Please and thank you. 😘
