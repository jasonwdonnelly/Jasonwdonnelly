I Was a Professional Juggler Before I Juggled Clients.

"I'm juggling a lot right now."

Everyone says it.

Almost nobody saying it has ever juggled.

I have. Professionally.

So let me ruin the metaphor for you a little. Then let me fix it.

The comfortable lie

Here's what people mean when they say they're juggling.

They mean they're busy.

They mean there's a lot going on and they're scared something will fall.

They picture themselves frantically grabbing at everything, all at once, eyes darting, sweat on the forehead.

That's not juggling.

That's panic with props.

But here's the thing

Real juggling is calm.

It has to be. The second you start grabbing, the pattern falls apart.

Juggling isn't about catching everything. It's about throwing well. And that one idea has done more for my career in advertising than most of the business books I've been handed.

Thirteen-plus years. VaynerMedia. iHeartMedia. 360i. Brands you've put in your fridge. A lot of clients, a lot of deadlines, a lot of things in the air at once.

The lessons that held up best didn't come from a conference.

They came from dropping things on a floor. Over and over.

Lesson one: the throw is the job

New jugglers obsess over the catch.

They watch their hands. They lunge. They try to save every bad throw at the last second.

But a good catch is just the receipt for a good throw.

If the throw is clean, the catch is boring. The object lands where you knew it would. Your hand barely moves.

If the throw is sloppy, no amount of heroic catching will save you for long.

Work is the same.

The brief is the throw. The kickoff is the throw. The first draft you hand to a client is the throw.

Most people spend their energy on the rescue. The late night. The last-minute rewrite. The "quick call" to smooth it over.

Spend it on the throw instead. Write the clearer brief. Ask the annoying question up front. Send the draft that actually says something.

Then the catch takes care of itself.

Lesson two: you can't watch everything

Here's the part nobody believes until they try it.

When you juggle, you don't stare at each object. You can't. There's always one more thing in the air than you have hands.

So you stop tracking every piece. You trust the pattern. You keep your eyes up, soft focus, and let your hands do what you trained them to do.

That is the whole secret to managing a heavy workload.

You cannot personally watch every project, every email, every Slack thread at the same time. If you try, you'll drop the one that matters while you're staring at the one that doesn't.

Build the pattern instead.

Systems. Rhythms. Templates. A weekly review. A team you actually trust.

Then stop staring at your hands.

Lesson three: drops are data

I'm going to hold your hands when I say this...

You will drop things.

Every juggler drops things. Every working professional drops things. The ones who get good aren't the ones who never drop. They're the ones who pick it up fast and ask why it fell.

Was the throw too far forward?

Too high?

Too rushed because I was nervous?

A drop isn't a verdict on you as a person. It's information. Ugly, embarrassing, very public information. But useful.

The worst thing you can do after a drop is pretend it didn't happen. The second worst is quitting.

Pick it up. Adjust the throw. Go again.

That's the whole method. For juggling. For copywriting. For running a brand, writing a novel, building a tiny AI tool that nobody asked for.

Lesson four: adding more is a skill, not a mood

Every juggler wants to add another object before they're ready.

It looks impressive. It feels like progress.

It's usually a disaster.

You add more when the current pattern is boring. Not before. When you could do it half asleep, while talking, while someone's heckling you. Then you add one.

Now look at how most of us take on work.

"Sure, I can take that project."

"Yeah, add me to that one too."

"Of course I'll lead that initiative."

Said by someone who is already dropping things.

More isn't a sign of skill. More is something you EARN by making your current load look easy.

So before you say yes to the next thing, ask yourself one honest question:

Is what I'm already holding boring yet?

If the answer is no, the answer to the new thing is probably no too.

Lesson five: the audience only sees the pattern

Nobody watching a juggler thinks about the hours of dropped practice.

They see the pattern. The flow. The thing that looks effortless.

That's the gig.

Clients don't want to see your process sweating on the table. Readers don't want to feel how hard the sentence was to write. Audiences want the clean pattern.

The work is making hard things look easy.

The trick is that "easy" is built entirely out of reps nobody saw.

What to actually do

So next time you catch yourself saying "I'm juggling a lot right now," try this instead.

Check your throws. Where are you creating your own messes up front? Fix the brief, not the fire.

Build the pattern. Stop watching every ball. Make systems you can trust with your eyes up.

Treat drops as data. Pick it up, figure out why, go again. No drama.

Earn the next ball. Only add more when your current load is boring.

Make it look easy. Then let people assume you were born that way. 🤪

The point

Juggling taught me that control isn't grabbing.

Control is a clean release.

You let go well, and you trust that you'll be there when it comes back down.

That's how I try to run every project I touch. If you want to see what that looks like when it lands, here's the work. Plenty of throws. A few catches I'm proud of.

And no, I will not juggle at your offsite.

Okay. Maybe once. 😘
