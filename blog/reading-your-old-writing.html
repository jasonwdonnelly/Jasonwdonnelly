Reading Your Own Old Writing Is a Special Kind of Pain. Do It Anyway.

Go find something you wrote five years ago.

A blog post. A cover letter. An ad. A short story you were very proud of at the time.

Read it out loud.

I'll wait. 🤪

The wince

You felt it, right?

That little full-body flinch. The urge to close the tab. The quiet voice going "who let this person have a keyboard?"

Most people treat that feeling like bad news. Like proof they were never any good. So they stop looking back. They keep the old stuff buried in a folder called "misc" and pretend it doesn't exist.

But here's the thing.

That wince is the best news you're going to get all year.

It means you can see something now that you couldn't see then. The flabby sentence. The joke that isn't a joke. The three adjectives doing the job of one good noun. The paragraph that exists only because you liked the sound of yourself.

You wrote all of that with a straight face.

Now you can't read it with one.

That gap between the two? That's taste. And it doesn't come from reading about writing. It comes from being embarrassed by your own writing, over and over, until the embarrassment starts showing up BEFORE you hit publish instead of after.

Taste is just accumulated damage

I've said this before and I'll keep saying it until someone puts it on a mug.

Taste is the accumulated damage of writing badly for years and learning to feel sick about it.

Nobody is born with it. You don't get it from a course. You don't get it from a clever framework in a carousel. (I make carousels. I know what they can and can't do.)

You get it by producing a lot of work, most of it mediocre, and then being brave enough to look at the mediocre stuff with clear eyes.

That last part is where people bail.

Producing the work is hard, sure. But looking at it later is harder. Because it's not a stranger's bad sentence. It's yours. It came out of your head. You thought it was good.

That's the special kind of pain.

Why this matters more right now

Here's what no one tells you about writing in the age of AI.

The machine will hand you a clean, competent, confident draft in about four seconds. It will never wince. It doesn't have old work it's ashamed of. It has no folder called "misc."

Which means it has no flinch.

And if YOU don't have one either, you'll look at that draft and think "yeah, looks fine." And you'll ship it. And it will sound like everything else on the internet, because it is everything else on the internet.

The people who get the most out of these tools are the ones who've built the flinch the hard way. They read the draft and something in their gut goes "no." They cut. They rebuild. They're the judgment in the room.

You can't outsource the flinch. You can only earn it.

And the fastest way to earn it is to go back and read your own old stuff until you can say exactly WHY it's bad.

How to actually do this (without spiraling)

I'm going to hold your hands when I say this...

Rereading your old work is not a punishment. It's a workout. So treat it like one.

Pick one piece. Not your whole archive. One thing. Something at least a couple of years old, so you have real distance.

Read it out loud. Your eyes will forgive things your mouth won't. If you run out of breath in a sentence, the sentence is too long. If you get bored, the reader got bored first.

Mark the wince. Every spot that makes you cringe, underline it. Don't fix anything yet. Just mark.

Name it. This is the part that actually builds taste. "This is bad" is useless. "This is bad because I buried the point in the third sentence" is a skill you now own forever. Try to name every wince. Too many words. Wrong word. Trying too hard. Hedging. Showing off. Explaining the joke.

Find the one good line. There's almost always one. A turn of phrase that still works. A moment where past-you got it right without knowing why. Circle it. That's your voice, trying to get out. Now you know what it sounds like.

Rewrite one paragraph. Just one. Not to publish. Just to feel the difference between who you were and who you are.

That's it. Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty.

Do it every few months and you'll watch your own taste develop in real time. Which is weird. And a little addictive.

The comfortable lie

The comfortable lie is that good writers look back at their early work fondly.

Methinks not.

Getting better means your old work has to look worse. That's the deal. The good ones keep that deal. They kept moving the bar and they kept being honest about where the old work fell under it.

The writers who love everything they've ever written? Those are the ones who stopped getting better.

If your old work doesn't make you wince, one of two things is true. Either you were a genius from day one (congratulations, please share your secrets) or you haven't grown.

I know which one I'd bet on.

What to do with the pain

Don't delete the old stuff.

Seriously. Keep it. It's a map of where you've been. It's proof you were bad, which is proof you got better, which is the only proof that matters when you're staring at a blank page and wondering if you can do this.

I have books out there with my name on them that I'd write differently today. That's not a regret. That's the whole point. They're a record of a writer figuring it out in public.

And the next thing I write? Future me is going to wince at that too.

Good.

That means he's still paying attention.

So go find something old. Read it out loud. Feel the pain.

Then write something new.

Write that down. 😘
