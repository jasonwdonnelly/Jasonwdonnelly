Satire Is the Only Honest Way Left to Tell the Truth.

Nobody argues with a joke.

That's the whole trick. That's the entire essay. You can stop reading now if you want, but you won't, because you can feel there's something under that sentence and you want to know what it is.

Here's what it is.

Tell someone the truth directly and they reach for a shield. Tell them the same truth inside a story that's clearly ridiculous, clearly exaggerated, clearly Not About Them, and they lower the shield to laugh.

And while the shield is down, the truth walks in.

The straight version gets deleted

I've spent 13+ years writing for brands. Big ones. Ones you keep in your fridge.

Here's something that job teaches you fast: the honest version of almost anything dies in review.

Not because anyone is evil. Because everyone is careful. Every layer of approval sands off a little more edge, until the thing that ships is smooth, safe, and says nothing. You've read a thousand of these. You read one this morning. You don't remember it, which is the point.

The truth, delivered straight, has too many stakeholders.

Say "our industry rewards pretending" in a meeting and watch the room do the polite freeze. Say it on LinkedIn and watch the comments fill with people explaining why THEIR pretending is different.

Direct truth triggers the immune system.

So the truth needs a disguise.

Fiction gets under the door

Satire is truth wearing a costume dumb enough to get past security.

A satirist doesn't say "you are like this." A satirist builds a character who is like that, cranks the dial until it's absurd, and lets you laugh at the character.

Then, somewhere around page forty, a quieter thought arrives.

Wait.

That's me.

Nobody can get defensive, because nobody was accused. You did the accusing yourself, privately, where it actually counts. That's the difference between satire and a callout post. A callout makes the reader defend. Satire makes the reader confess.

One changes minds. The other collects likes from people who already agreed.

I have skin in this game

I wrote a satirical novel about social media marketing called Gripped. The subtitle is "Your Personality Is What's Holding You Back," which should tell you exactly how gently it treats the industry I work in.

Methinks not gently.

Here's the thing though. Everything I wanted to say in that book, I could have said in a tidy little thought-leadership post. "The metrics are hollow. The personal brands are costumes. We all know it."

Nobody needs that post. Everyone has already scrolled past that post.

But make it a story? Make it absurd, make it funny, make it fiction? Now the reader isn't being lectured. The reader is watching. And a reader who is watching relaxes in a way a reader being lectured never does.

Fiction doesn't just get under the door. Fiction gets invited in.

And it doesn't have to be funny to do it. Five Stitches, my second novel, is dark. No winking. But it works the same muscle: a made-up story that lets you sit with something true for longer than you'd tolerate if I said it to your face.

That's the real power of fiction. Not escape.

Extended eye contact with the truth.

Why this matters more right now

We are drowning in direct statements.

Everyone is telling you the truth in bold fonts. Hot takes, contrarian threads, "unpopular opinions" that forty thousand people share because they are extremely popular. Sincerity became a format, and formats get gamed. Half the vulnerability in your feed was reverse-engineered from an engagement chart.

So audiences adapted. They flinch at anything that announces itself as honest. The phrase "let me be real with you for a second" now sets off every alarm the reader has.

Straight talk has a credibility problem, and it earned it.

Satire doesn't announce anything. It's clearly a bit. It leads with the joke and smuggles the thesis. The reader's guard stays down because nothing on the surface looks like persuasion.

Which means the writers who can do it hold the last unlocked door.

The catch, because there's always a catch

Satire is not a free pass to be mean.

Punching down isn't satire, it's bullying with a thesaurus. Real satire punches up, or better, punches inward. The best satire of an industry comes from someone inside it, someone implicating themselves with every page.

I write satire about marketing while working in marketing. That's not hypocrisy.

That's the qualification.

If the joke doesn't cost you something, it's not satire. It's just content.

What to actually do with this

You don't need to write a novel. (You can. I'll wait.) But you can borrow the mechanism tomorrow.

Next time you have a truth nobody wants to hear, at work, in your content, wherever, stop drafting the direct version. Build a character instead. Describe "a company" that does the thing. Write the parody version of the email, the meeting, the launch plan. Exaggerate until it's funny.

Then watch people laugh, pause, and get quiet.

That quiet is the truth landing. No shield ever went up.

The straight version would have gotten you an argument. The absurd version got you a confession.

Write that down.

And if you want to see what happens when I aim that machine at my own industry, Gripped and Five Stitches are both right here. One will make you laugh at marketing. The other will just make you feel things.

Choose your fighter. 😘
