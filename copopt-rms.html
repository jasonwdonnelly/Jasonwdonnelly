G04A draft → A positionTask 3 · writing exercise

The writing sample: one post, under 300 words

A LinkedIn post, operator-truth voice, on the manual-rebuild insight.

LinkedIn · Copenhagen Optimization · ~210 words

One of our customers told us that explaining our software to a colleague felt like putting himself out of a job.

That line should worry every vendor in our category. It doesn't. We put it on a slide.

Before this airport found a system that worked, one person spent seven days a week plotting resources by hand. Counters. Gates. Baggage. Makeup units. Every week. The moment one week's plan was published, the next one started.

They searched seventeen years for something better. Not because they lacked budget. Because nothing on the market could handle the mess of a real hub operation.

Here's what no one in this category likes to admit. Airports don't struggle to make a plan. They struggle because the plan dies the second the day starts moving. A flight lands early. A belt breaks. And a person rebuilds the whole thing by hand. Again.

There's a name for that. The manual rebuild.

Most vendors sell you a faster one and call it "intelligent, real-time optimization." We don't think the smartest thing this software can do is run the rebuild faster.

It's to make sure no one runs it again.

He didn't lose his job. He got it back. The part that needs a human. On the floor, with the airlines, on the exceptions a machine shouldn't touch.

That's the end of the manual rebuild.

G05Generation → JudgmentTask 4 · AI usage

How AI was used, and where it wasn't trusted

My first instinct with AI isn't to generate. It's to distrust. Before I built anything, I used it to check what I'd been handed.

I ran the source pack against itself and the public record, across two models (Claude and ChatGPT), because the fastest way to catch a confident machine is to make another one disagree with it. That caught two things.

Catch 01 · the stat that fights itself

The nurture flow cites a Denver result of "33%" and "95% automation." Your own published case study says 30% and "near-total." A small gap, but it's your number, in your funnel, contradicting itself.

Catch 02 · the confident upgrade

I asked AI to verify a spokesperson's credential. It promoted her from "Advisory Board member" to "Chair of the Advisory Board." Real person, real board, real-sounding, wrong. Neither model flagged it; a human cross-reference did.

That's the whole lesson: AI handed me a credible, specific, false detail and felt great about it. The value wasn't the answer, it was not trusting the answer.

Leaned on it for

  • Reading the full document pack in one pass
  • Pressure-testing my logic, arguing the other side
  • Generating options to react to
  • Building. This site was built AI-native, in days.

Wouldn't hand it

  • The strategic bets: champion-first, the contrarian spine, naming the manual rebuild
  • Any fact, unverified
  • The voice. It drafts confident, forgettable copy all day.
  • Reading the room: how hard to critique your own marketing

Not a chatbot. Purpose-built skills.

The leverage wasn't in prompting a chat window. I ran the work through specialized AI skills I'd already built: a content-strategist editor that re-audited the deck against the brief, swept it for repetition, and forced the cuts that took it from twelve sections to six, and a voice skill trained on my own writing that drafted the LinkedIn sample so it sounds like me, not like a model. That's the difference between using AI and building with it: the judgment is encoded in the tools, not borrowed from the default.

One more call AI would never make for me. I first built a full company-wide content hub with AI in a few days, then decided not to use it: it was scoped to the whole company, not RMS, and it was exactly the polished artifact this brief de-emphasizes. Knowing when the impressive output is the wrong move is also judgment. That hub is viewable here, as process, not product.

Where AI scales the engine, going forward

The brief also asks where AI scales the operation. This is what makes the North Star realistic: localizing at grand scale only works because AI does the scaling, one interview into a week of cuts in multiple languages, a GEO page factory of definitional and comparison pages. The line that doesn't move: AI drafts, repurposes, and scales, but never owns the fact-check, the bet, or the voice. Scale without judgment is just slop, faster.

The win: AI is how one strategist builds a global network's worth of content. It didn't make a single decision in this deck. That was the point.

Seat upgrade · your five debates, answered

  • Thought leadership vs product marketing? Insights, and real voices. You aren't a product, you're the authority on how airports run. Lead with what the best operators see, not a feature list. That connection is the content.
  • Awareness vs demand? Not a trade-off, a sequence. Awareness is the proof you actually do this. Demand is what happens the moment a buyer believes it.

  • Customer stories vs expert-led? A false choice. The customers are the experts. The best expert-led content is the customer's story, told by the person who lived it. Same person.
  • AI search visibility? A no-brainer. Make content do double work: written for the operator, structured for the machine, so when a buyer asks an LLM who's best at airport operations, the answer is Copenhagen Optimization, by name.
