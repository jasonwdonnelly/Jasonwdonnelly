00 Start here The Brief What Too Good To Go actually asked for, in plain English.

Too Good To Go wants to lean less on paid ads and grow the organic way: by winning back the pile of people who heard of it years ago, maybe tried it once, then drifted. The job is one brand campaign built on a single idea, stretched across a manifesto, billboards, an email and social, with a short rationale. It's a behavioral nudge, not a discount-and-download conversion push.

Objective

Foster organic growth and re-engagement. Ease reliance on paid UA, which loses efficiency as markets mature.

Audience

The Conscious Shopper. Urban, 25–35. Lapsed, aware non-users.

The insight

Shift surplus from "stigmatized leftovers" to amazing things waiting to be discovered.

Main message

There's an abundance of good stuff waiting with Too Good To Go. (Express it, don't repeat it.)

Tone

Witty not sarcastic. Playful not childish. Confident not arrogant. Rebellious not provocative.

What they don't want

No lower-funnel tactics. No niche-activist positioning. No charity or food-insecurity framing.

The trap

The brief says brand campaign, not conversion, then lists "registrations and first orders rise" as a goal. Weak entries sneak in promo codes and "download now." This one holds the brand line and lets the idea do the converting.

01 The platform The Idea: "Meet Your Maker" Surplus food isn't stigmatized because it's old. It's stigmatized because it's anonymous.
People don't avoid surplus food because it's bad. They avoid it because it's nobody's.

"Leftovers" is what we call food with no author. It belongs to no one, so no one misses it. But no food is actually anonymous. Somebody proofed that dough at four in the morning. Somebody rolled that rice at eleven. Give the food its maker back and the stigma has nowhere to live.

So the unit of this campaign isn't an ad. It's an episode: fifteen seconds, a face, a name, a craft, one dry line, end card. Every shop is its own hero, in its own neighborhood, in its own words. Everything else in this deck (the billboards, the email, all of it) is that episode, re-expressed. The pun is played completely straight; the phrase brings its own dark little grin and we never once acknowledge it.

Meet Your Maker.
Authorless "leftovers" ↓ Somebody's best work. Signed.

No casting hierarchy, no hero food, no campaign clock. Every shop is its own hero episode. The footage flows to TGTG, gets our branding, and runs on our channels city by city; the shop gets its own cut back for its own promotion. See sections 05 and 06.

Too Good To Go works with 180,000+ food businesses in 21 countries. Most campaigns are one idea stretched thin. This one is 180,000 introductions that haven't happened yet.

Guardrail

The maker is never a victim. The second this campaign asks anyone to feel sorry for a baker, it dies and becomes the charity ad the brief banned. Makers are proud, mildly annoyed, and right. Nobody here needs saving. The food just needs eating.

The two cuts  ·  footage flows in, both cuts flow out

Every episode is edited twice. Identical fifteen seconds; only the last frame changes. The footage is sent to us; we make both cuts.

The TGTG cut · the campaign

Ends on the Meet Your Maker end card. Published on our channels, our logo, our voice, localized city by city so Copenhagen sees Copenhagen's makers and Lyon sees Lyon's. The brand's organic reach grows with every episode, and paid enters last, boosting only what's already proven.

The shop's cut · the thank-you

Same film, ends on their name, hours, and "come by at closing." Theirs to post and promote, free advertising they would never budget for. It's the perk that makes sending footage in a no-brainer, not the distribution plan.

Why a format beats a film A film gets watched. A format gets copied. The campaign opens with a public ask to every partner: introduce yourself, send it in. Brand crews shoot the flagship episodes in each market to set the bar; every other shop answers with the kit, and the footage comes to us. TGTG brands and publishes every episode on its own channels, localized to the shop's city, so the campaign builds the brand's organic reach instead of renting attention. Best answers earn the crew visit, so being filmed becomes a status thing among partners. Brand asks → shops send → we publish, our name on it → paid boosts only proven winners. That's the lean-off-paid-UA answer, built into the creative itself.

06 The organic engine The Ask The campaign launches as an open call: introduce yourself.

This campaign doesn't distribute creative to shops. It asks them for something: introduce yourself. A public, open call to every partner, all at once. The ask itself is the launch event (the world's largest introduction, and the press writes that headline for us), the brand-shot flagships answer first to set the bar, and the kit below is simply how everyone else answers. Partners already post; they have phones, regulars, and pride. The kit doesn't create that behavior, it formats the answer. Most campaigns rent a film crew. This one comes with 180,000 of them.

The 3-shot list

  1. Your face, where you work. Look at the camera.
  2. Your hands, doing the thing you're best at.
  3. The bag, at closing. Hand it to someone if you can.

The prompt card

Say your name. Say what you make. Then tell us the one thing customers get wrong about the end of your day.

The two tiers

  1. Brand-shot flagships. A lean crew films hero shops per market. Two cuts each: the shop's and ours. These carry the localized paid pushes.
  2. Kit-shot, everyone else. Shops film themselves and send it in. TGTG brands and publishes on its own channels; top performers earn the crew visit.

07 Deliverable 03 · Rationale Why this, and why it works In my voice, under 250 words.

Every surplus campaign makes the same mistake: it markets the food. The food was never the problem.

Surplus feels sad because it feels authorless. A bag of "leftovers" belongs to nobody, so nobody misses it. But no food is actually anonymous. Somebody proofed that dough at four in the morning. Somebody rolled that rice at eleven. Give the food its maker back and "stigmatized leftovers" becomes impossible. You can dismiss surplus. It's much harder to dismiss Kasper.

That's the mechanism: anonymity is the stigma. Fix one, the other goes.

So I didn't write a film. I wrote an ask: introduce yourself. Fifteen seconds: face, name, craft, one dry line, end card. Every shop answers as its own hero, in its own neighborhood. And the footage flows to us. We brand it and run it on our channels, city by city, organically. Their cut comes back to them, free advertising they'd never budget for. Partners aren't doing us a favor. They're getting an ad out of it. That's why it scales. Paid boosts only what's proven.

On tone: the pun does the rebellious work, so everything else plays it straight. Deadpan, warm, zero violins. The makers are proud, mildly annoyed, and right, which is the exact register of every great baker I've ever met.

And the don'ts hold. No codes, no activism, no charity. Just the oldest sales technique, an introduction, aimed at dinner. You show up differently for a person than for a price.

08 For the walk-through How I'd defend it in the room The five questions a creative director will ask, hardest first.

"Will shops actually make these?"

Wrong worry. The shop's cut is free advertising they'd never budget for, made about the thing they're proudest of. Shops will compete to be episodes. The kit formats behavior that already exists, the end card keeps it ours, and recut rights mean we never police a hundred thousand edits.

"Why does a lapsed user care who made it?"

Because anonymity is the stigma. "Leftovers" is the word for food with no author. Once the author shows up, the dismissal stops working, and a person is harder to swipe past than a price.

"Isn't this just every craft-food ad?"

Craft ads point pride at the 9 a.m. shelf. We point the same pride at closing time, the exact moment the whole category is embarrassed about. Nobody else is standing there, which is what makes it ownable.

"Where's the nudge?"

An introduction creates a small social pull that a discount never does. You show up differently for a person than for a price. That's the behavioral mechanism, and it converts without a single promo code in sight.

"You do know what that phrase means, right?"

Yes. That's the joke, and we never tell it. Played straight, the dark read is a smirk the audience gets to have all by themselves. Acknowledged even once, it becomes a brand being edgy. "Rebellious, not provocative" comes down to who's allowed to wink: here it's them, never us.
